How Long Does It Take to Build a Custom Superyacht?

SUPERYACHT NEW BUILDS  ·  10 min read

Construction on Moonrise began in 2021. She was launched in May 2025 and delivered that July — four years from first steel to handover, for a 101-metre Feadship built by a yard that does nothing but flagships. The owner who commissioned her had waited through a pandemic-era supply chain, a war in Europe and a labour market that every northern European yard was fighting over. Four years, in that context, was not a delay. It was the schedule.

That is the number buyers of custom superyachts need to understand before they sign anything. The question is not how long the build takes. The question is how long the wait takes — and the two numbers are set by different people, years apart.

The short answer

A fully custom superyacht takes three to four years to build, according to Burgess, which puts the design phase alone at nine months to a year on average. Moran Yacht & Ship, the American brokerage and new-build consultancy, gives a similar shape with a wider band: most fully custom builds take two to four years to complete, with design development at three to six months, hull construction at six to twelve, and final testing and delivery at two to four. Size moves the number more than anything else. A planning guide from GlobalYachtGuide puts a full-custom motor yacht of 30 to 50 metres at 24 to 42 months from contract, and a full-custom superyacht over 50 metres at 36 to 60 months. A 45-metre motor yacht at a quality European yard runs 30 to 42 months from first brief to delivery, on one detailed build timeline; the same timeline puts a vessel bought at an advanced stage of speculative construction at 18 to 24 months.

Those are contract-to-delivery figures. The owner’s clock starts earlier — brief, designer search, yard selection and contract negotiation run four to eight months before construction starts — and ends later, with sea trials, snagging and handover. From first conversation to first cruise, a large custom superyacht is a four-to-five-year commitment, and anyone quoting less is usually quoting the yard’s build time, not the owner’s waiting time.

Phase by phase

The build moves through five broad phases, and the first is the one owners rush and later regret. Brief and designer selection takes one to six months: the owner’s requirements, the choice of exterior designer and naval architect. The names at this end of the market are a short list — Espen Øino, Winch Design, H2 Yacht Design, Tim Heywood among them — and the good ones are booked. Design development runs six to eighteen months and overlaps construction rather than preceding it: detailed engineering continues while early structural work is already under way, and systems installation begins before the interior design is fully resolved. That overlap is why late design changes are so expensive. A change made while the hull is still a drawing costs a meeting. The same change made while the hull is in the shed costs a season.

Shipyard selection and contracting takes four to eight months, and the contract is where the timeline becomes enforceable: build specification, staged payments against construction milestones, delivery date, penalty provisions for late delivery, and the owner’s rights if the yard fails. Then construction, months twelve to thirty-six: hull and superstructure first, mechanical and electrical systems next, interior outfitting last — with hundreds of subcontractors and thousands of decisions in between. Launch, commissioning and sea trials run months thirty to forty-two. Sea trials are not a ceremonial lap. They are a full performance verification, conducted with the builder, naval architect, surveyors and an independent technical representative present: speed trials against the contracted maximum, stability tests, endurance runs at operating loads. For commercially operated yachts over 24 metres, certification follows the Red Ensign Group Yacht Code, which sets the construction, safety and equipment standards a vessel must meet before it can be issued a certificate. Delivery — formal transfer of ownership — follows successful trials and final snagging. Professional services across the project, including design, naval architecture, engineering and classification support, account for roughly 7 to 10 percent of total build cost.

The waitlist sets the date

None of that timetable matters if there is no slot to build in. BOAT International’s Global Order Book 2026, published in December 2025, counts 1,093 superyachts of 24 metres or more on order or in build, for a combined 44,637 metres — down from 1,138 projects a year earlier, a decline of about 4 percent, while average length rose to 40.8 metres and average gross tonnage to a record 551 GT. Italy builds 568 of those projects, about 52 percent of the world total, with 22,299 metres under construction. Azimut-Benetti tops the builder ranking for the twenty-sixth successive year with 163 yachts totalling 5,924 metres; Sanlorenzo is second at 130 yachts and 4,698 metres. The largest yachts concentrate elsewhere: 67 projects exceed 76 metres and 26 exceed 100 metres, and the average length under construction reaches 65.8 metres in the Netherlands and 100 metres in Germany.

The consequence is visible in the delivery calendar. Build slots at leading yards are committed through 2028 and 2029, according to the Monaco Yacht Show’s 2026 fleet briefing — which means a buyer who signs a contract in late 2026 is, at the best yards, queuing for a slot two to three years out before construction even starts. The order book has contracted in unit count for a second consecutive year while average size and tonnage reach record levels: fewer yachts, bigger yachts, longer queues for the yards that build them. A yard with a four-year forward order book cannot deliver a vessel in thirty months, whatever its proposal says.

Real delivery schedules

The 2025 and 2026 delivery lists show what those timelines produce in practice. Feadship delivered three headline yachts in 2025: the 118.8-metre Breakthrough, launched in 2024; the 101-metre Moonrise, construction begun in 2021, launched May 2025, delivered that July; and the 100-metre Pi. All three are full-custom builds, all three took years from contract to handover, and all three were delivered into a market where the brokerage alternative was scarce — which is why they matter to pricing as well as to patience.

The 2026 list continues the pattern. Lürssen delivered the 114.2-metre, 6,593-gross-tonne Project Cosmos in May 2026, and the 102-metre Lürssen Nixie was among the 43 yachts delivered in 2026 and shown at the Monaco Yacht Show that September. Feadship, with 18 yachts in build or on order, was due to deliver the 101.2-metre, 3,840-gross-tonne Project 1014 in 2026. These are not assembly-line outputs. Each is a single project with its own design team, its own subcontractor chain and its own sea-trial programme — and each delivery date was set years earlier, when the slot was booked.

The case for not waiting

There is a serious argument for refusing the wait entirely, and the brokerage market supplies it. A vessel bought at an advanced stage of speculative construction — a hull the yard started on its own account — delivers in 18 to 24 months, roughly half the time of a full custom build, and the trade is clear: the buyer accepts the yard’s design decisions in exchange for speed. The brokerage market offers a faster answer still. Article 5 in this series recorded 416 superyachts sold in the first nine months of 2026, with the median sale spending 317 days on the market — under a year from listing to closing for a yacht that already exists.

The sharpest illustration is a yacht called Moonrise. Not the 101-metre Feadship delivered in 2025, but her predecessor: the 99.95-metre Feadship delivered in 2020, also called Moonrise, built for WhatsApp co-founder Jan Koum. She entered the brokerage market in January 2026 at a €325 million asking price, changed hands that July — Burgess representing the seller, Edmiston the buyer — and was renamed Reverie. A buyer who wanted a 100-metre Feadship in 2026 could have waited four years for a new one, or bought that one in seven months. The new build offers exactly the yacht the owner wants. The brokerage purchase offers the yacht this decade. For a buyer in his sixties, that is not a trivial distinction.

Why dates slip

Even contracted dates slip, and the industry’s own accounts say so plainly. Budget overruns of 15 to 30 percent are reported industry-wide, according to GlobalYachtGuide, and delivery delays of 12 to 24 months are common enough that insiders treat them as the default rather than the exception. The global supply-chain disruption of 2020 to 2023 pushed many new builds six to eighteen months beyond their original delivery dates, and the causes have not gone away: late design changes, material procurement, subcontractor coordination, and owner-driven scope additions. The commissioning phase — launch, systems testing, sea trials — routinely takes longer than planned, because modern superyacht systems are complex and the subcontractors whose work must be coordinated in sequence are numerous.

None of this means the yards are unreliable. It means the product is a prototype. Every custom superyacht is the first of its kind, built once, by a temporary coalition of firms that will never work in exactly that combination again — with the owner changing the specification halfway through. The surprise would be if it arrived on time.

What to put in the contract

Buy the slot, not the brochure. The single most valuable clause in a new-build contract is the delivery date with penalties attached, and the second is the sea-trial acceptance sequence — agreed at signing, not negotiated near delivery, when the owner’s leverage is gone. Stage payments against verified construction milestones, not calendar dates. Insist on an independent technical representative at sea trials, with the right to refuse acceptance if the yacht misses contracted performance. And budget the delay: if the contract says 2029, plan for 2030, and decide in advance what the owner does in the intervening year — because the answer is usually a brokerage purchase or a charter, and both are cheaper arranged early.

The wait is the price of getting exactly what was asked for, and for the buyers at the top of this market it is a price they pay gladly. Moonrise took four years. Her owner took delivery in July 2025 and sailed her to Norway on her maiden voyage. The buyers who cannot wait buy brokerage, and the buyers who can wait buy new — and the order book, slots committed through 2029, suggests there are more of the second kind than there are slots for them.
