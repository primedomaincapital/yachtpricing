Yacht Charter vs. Ownership: The Math at 30 Days a Year

YACHT CHARTER · 7 min read

The owner had done the arithmetic the way owners do it: he had not done it. He had bought a 100-foot yacht for $10 million, used it for six weeks a year, and discovered — in the third year, when the refit estimate arrived — that the boat cost him about $1.2 million a year to run. That is $200,000 a week for the six weeks he actually used it, against a charter rate of $80,000 to $180,000 a week for the same boat, crewed, fueled, and provisioned. He had bought the boat because he loved it. He was not wrong to love it. He was wrong about what it cost, and the error was not a small one. It was a factor of two.

The rule of thumb in yachting is the 10 percent rule: a yacht costs about 10 percent of its purchase price every year to operate. A $10 million yacht costs about $1 million a year. A $20 million yacht costs about $2 million. The rule is rough, but it is honest, and it is the number that should be on the first page of every purchase decision. The breakdown, from the Superyacht Report's operating-expenditure survey of the 28-to-83-metre fleet, is crew 35 percent, insurance 12 percent, maintenance 10 percent, dockage 5 percent, fuel 8 percent, refit reserve 15 percent, and miscellaneous 15 percent. Crew is the largest line, and it is the line that never shrinks: a 100-foot yacht needs a captain, a mate, a chef, and a stewardess, and they are paid whether the boat moves or not. The refit reserve is the line that surprises owners most, because it is the line that arrives all at once — a five-year survey, a paint job, an engine overhaul — and it is 15 percent of the annual budget, every year, whether or not anything breaks this year.

The charter side of the ledger

Charter rates for a 100-foot yacht run $80,000 to $180,000 a week for the base rate, depending on the age, the condition, and the season — one broker's range for 80-to-100-foot yachts is $40,000 to $120,000, and the difference between the two ranges is the difference between a ten-year-old boat and a new one. Larger yachts scale fast: 150-to-180-foot yachts charter for $250,000 to $550,000 a week, and 60-metre-plus yachts for $500,000 to $1.2 million and more. The base rate is not the total. The Advance Provisioning Allowance — the APA — adds 25 to 35 percent on top, covering fuel, food, port fees, and consumables, and the crew gratuity adds another 15 to 20 percent. A $100,000-a-week base rate becomes $145,000 to $155,000 all-in, and the charterer pays it only for the weeks they actually use. There is no crew to pay in January. There is no refit reserve. There is no dockage bill for a boat that is sitting in a marina in February, waiting for summer.

Thirty days a year is the number that matters, because thirty days is the usage level where the comparison stops being obvious. At 30 days — roughly four and a half weeks — chartering a 100-foot yacht at $100,000 a week costs about $450,000, plus the APA and gratuity, for a total near $600,000. Owning the same yacht costs $1.2 million a year, before depreciation. The charterer is ahead by $600,000, and the charterer has no asset to maintain, no crew to employ, and no depreciation to absorb. Depreciation is the line owners forget: 5 to 8 percent a year for the first ten years, which on a $10 million yacht is $500,000 to $800,000 a year in lost value, on top of the $1.2 million in operating costs. The all-in cost of ownership at 30 days a year is closer to $1.7 to $2 million. The charterer pays $600,000 and walks away.

Where the break-even actually sits

The break-even — the usage level at which ownership becomes cheaper than chartering — is the number every article in this genre tries to pin down, and the honest answer is that the published estimates disagree by a factor of three. One analysis puts it at four to eight weeks a year. Another puts it above ten to twelve weeks. The range itself is the story, because the break-even depends on assumptions that are never the same twice: the purchase price, the charter rate for the equivalent boat, the depreciation schedule, and whether the owner charters the boat out when they are not using it. The Superyacht Report's survey found that typical owners use their yachts four to eight weeks a year, committed users use them 12 to 16 weeks, and owners are aboard an average of 17 weeks a year — a number that includes the owner's own use plus the weeks the boat is chartered out. At four to eight weeks, chartering wins. At 12 to 16 weeks, ownership starts to win. At 17 weeks, ownership wins clearly — but 17 weeks is a usage level that very few owners reach, and it is a usage level that requires chartering the boat out, which changes the economics entirely.

Chartering the boat out is the owner's best defense, and it is a partial one. Charter income offsets 10 to 30 percent of running costs in the first year, rising to 40 to 60 percent by year two or three, as the boat builds a charter record and a broker network. But the case studies from BOAT International, published in April 2025, show how thin the margin is: a 48-metre motor yacht that chartered for seven weeks earned €1,592,000 net against €1,575,000 in running costs — a break-even, after seven weeks of strangers on the boat. A 47-metre sailing yacht that chartered for nine weeks lost €444,000. An 85-metre motor yacht that chartered for eight weeks lost €430,000. Chartering out covers the costs only when the boat is large enough, new enough, and busy enough — and even then, it barely covers them. The owner who buys a yacht to charter it out is buying a business, and the business loses money in most of the cases that get published.

The counterargument is that the math is not the point, and the owners know it. A yacht is not an investment. It is a place, a way of spending time with family, and a thing that cannot be replicated by chartering, because chartering means a different boat every time, a different crew every time, and a boat that is never quite yours. The market agrees: sales of yachts over 24 metres reached $8.5 billion in 2025, up 70 percent year over year, according to BOAT International via the Knight Frank Wealth Report 2026. The buyers are not confused about the economics. They are buying something the economics do not measure. The honest advice is not to tell them not to buy. It is to tell them what the boat will cost, so that the decision is made with the real number in front of them: $1.2 million a year to run, $500,000 to $800,000 a year in depreciation, and a break-even that sits somewhere between four and twelve weeks, depending on assumptions that are never the same twice. If the answer is still yes, the boat is worth buying. If the answer depends on the boat paying for itself, it is not.
