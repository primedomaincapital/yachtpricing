What Does It Cost to Run a 100-Foot Yacht for a Year?

YACHT RUNNING COSTS  ·  10 min read

The statement arrived in February, four months after the closing. The owner had paid a little under $12 million for a 100-foot motor yacht, a brokerage purchase he had negotiated hard and closed feeling pleased with himself. Then the management company sent the first annual account: crew, fuel, berth, insurance, maintenance, administration. The total sat just above $1.4 million, and none of it was optional. The yacht had spent the winter tied up in the Mediterranean, barely moving, and it had still cost more in four quiet months than most people spend on a house in a year. He called his broker with the question every first-time owner eventually asks: is this normal?

It is. A 100-foot yacht is the point where yachting stops being a hobby with a mooring bill and becomes a small business with a payroll. The purchase price is the number everyone negotiates. The running cost decides whether the owner still enjoys the boat in year three.

The headline number

Compiled 2026 estimates put a 100-foot yacht at $8 million to $25 million to buy and $1 million to $2.5 million a year to run — roughly $85,000 to $210,000 a month once crew, dockage, insurance, fuel and maintenance are counted, or about 8 to 12 percent of hull value a year. Those are YachtMarketIntel’s compiled figures, built from brokerage transaction data, and they are the most commonly quoted shape of the answer. Other compilations land lower: the same publisher’s bottom-up table puts a 100-footer at $650,000 to $1.7 million a year, with crew at $250,000 to $500,000. The spread is the first honest fact about this market. There is no survey of what 100-foot yachts actually cost to run, because owners do not publish their accounts. Every number in this article is a compiled estimate or a broker benchmark, and the ranges below are wider than a buyer would like precisely because the underlying data is thin.

The rule of thumb every broker quotes is 10 percent: budget a tenth of the yacht’s value each year. Burgess still quotes 10 percent as a rule of thumb for yachts under 50 metres. Fraser puts the benchmark at 10 to 15 percent of purchase value. The number is a sanity check, not a budget. At a Monaco Yacht Show ownership panel in October 2026, Ryan Green, Burgess’s head of yacht management, told the room that 10 percent of value a year was “a very dangerous marker to put in the sand.” The same panel put a modern 40-metre yacht — roughly 130 feet — at £1.4 million to £1.8 million a year to run, which is 12 to 15 percent of a typical value for that size. The people who pay the bills use a band, not a number: 10 to 15 percent, with the top of the band for older boats, heavy use and Mediterranean home ports.

Crew: the bill that never stops

Crew is the largest line in the budget, and the one that never takes a season off. A 100-foot yacht typically runs with five to eight professionals — captain, engineer, chef, stewardess, deckhands — at $500,000 to $900,000 a year all-in, according to YachtMarketIntel’s 100-foot breakdown. Crew runs 35 to 40 percent of total annual spending, the Monaco panel’s own account.

The captain’s pay is the most documented figure in yachting, because crew agencies publish it. YPI Crew’s March 2026 salary guide puts a captain on an 80-to-100-metre yacht at €16,000 to €23,000 a month; a 50-metre captain earns €10,000 to €16,000. Quay Crew’s 2025/26 survey of 367 captains, measuring actual pay rather than guide bands, found averages past 100 metres running over €25,000 a month. A 100-foot yacht sits at the bottom of that scale — a captain in the region of $130,000 to $180,000 a year before benefits is a realistic planning figure, with an engineer and a chef each earning a substantial fraction of it. Then come the costs that do not appear on the salary line: social charges, health insurance, flights home between rotations, training certificates that expire whether the yacht moves or not, and the year-end bonus — customarily a month’s salary — that the industry treats as obligatory. Cut crew pay and the saving lasts about six months — then the good captain leaves, and the owner pays a recruitment fee and a season of mistakes to replace them.

Fuel: the variable nobody controls

Fuel is the line that separates a spreadsheet from a season. A 100-foot motor yacht burns roughly 50 to 100 gallons an hour at cruising speed, and compiled estimates put the annual fuel bill at $200,000 to $400,000 for a normal cruising programme. Most owners log 200 to 500 engine hours a year, and Mediterranean marine diesel runs about $1.00 to $1.50 a litre. Speed is the lever nobody admits to pulling. A 50-metre displacement yacht burns around 200 litres an hour at 12 knots; push a 60-metre performance yacht to 25 knots and consumption can reach 800 to 1,200 litres an hour. A 100-footer is smaller than either, but the physics is identical: every extra knot costs disproportionately more fuel, and the owner who cruises at 20 knots pays for it twice — once at the pump, once in engine hours that bring the refit forward.

Fuel is also the cost the owner has least control over after the decision to own. Diesel prices move with oil markets and local taxes. A summer spent crossing between the Balearics and Sardinia costs multiples of a summer spent at anchor off one island. Budget fuel by hours and speed, not by last year’s bill.

Berthing: geography is a price

If crew is the largest bill, berthing is the most visible one. Compiled estimates put an annual berth for a 100-footer at $80,000 to $200,000 in a normal Mediterranean marina. Monaco is not a normal marina. Annual berths in Port Hercule run $300,000 to more than $1 million, and Burgess’s own buying guide puts Monaco and Portofino berthing north of €5,000 a night in high season. In the harbours that matter, a berth is a scarce asset allocated years in advance, and some owners hold annual berths in two locations to guarantee a place in each season.

The berth decision is really three decisions: where the yacht winters, where it spends the summer, and where it can be hauled out. Winter berths are cheaper and easier. Summer berths in the Côte d’Azur, the Balearics or the Amalfi Coast are the expensive ones, and the price is set by length, beam and season, not by what the yacht cost. A 100-foot yacht with a wide beam pays more than a narrow one of the same length.

Insurance and maintenance: the quiet lines

Insurance for a superyacht typically runs 1 to 2 percent of the yacht’s value a year, according to YATCO — $200,000 to $400,000 on a $20 million vessel, and $100,000 to $250,000 on a 100-footer in the compiled ranges. The premium moves with cruising area, claims history, crew qualifications and whether the yacht charters; a claim reprices the following year.

Maintenance is the line owners underestimate most consistently, because most of it is invisible until it is not. Compiled guidance allocates 3 to 5 percent of the yacht’s value a year to routine maintenance alone, with a further 1 to 2 percent accrued toward the eventual refit. On a 100-footer that is $150,000 to $400,000 a year for maintenance and the annual haul-out, before anything breaks. A single refit every five to seven years can run into the millions on a larger yacht, YATCO notes, and the five-year class survey is a six-figure event on its own.

Adding it up

Build a mid-case from the ranges above and the shape of the answer is clear. Take a 100-foot yacht worth about $12 million, run properly with a full crew and a Mediterranean programme. Crew, $650,000. Fuel, $300,000. Berth, $140,000. Insurance, $175,000. Maintenance and haul-out, $275,000. The total is about $1.54 million a year — just under 13 percent of value, at the top of the 10-to-15-percent band, and inside the $1 million-to-$2.5 million compiled range. That is arithmetic from published ranges, not a surveyed figure, and it should be read as the middle of a wide distribution: a lean operation with a smaller crew and a home port outside the premium harbours can run meaningfully below it, and an older yacht, heavily used, with a Monaco berth, can run well above it.

The mid-case leaves out depreciation — a further 5 to 10 percent a year for the first five years, or $600,000 to $1.2 million a year on a $12 million yacht — and the refit reserve of 1 to 2 percent a year that a disciplined owner accrues and an undisciplined one discovers all at once in year five.

The case for the lower number

There is a serious argument that $1 million a year is too high, and it deserves a hearing. An owner-captain with three or four crew instead of six, a cheaper home port, a short season and a light cruising programme can run a 100-footer for far less — the bottom-up tables that put the total at $650,000 to $1.7 million describe exactly that owner. Charter income can offset more: a 100-footer in a well-managed charter programme earns real revenue in the summer weeks the owner is not aboard.

Both offsets have a price. A smaller crew means the owner is the crew, at least some of the time. Charter means wear — hundreds of extra engine hours, more guests through the interior, a commercial survey regime and a management percentage. And the owner who spends least on maintenance owns the yacht that loses most value. Article 3 in this series noted that 100-foot-plus yachts carry a $5 million-to-$8 million annual operating reality — a figure for larger superyachts, not 100-footers, but a reminder that every step up in length moves the cost curve faster than the length does. The cheapest way to run a 100-foot yacht is to run it less. The second cheapest is to buy a newer one.

What the first year teaches

Budget 12 percent, not 10, in the first year — and hold a reserve equal to a full year’s maintenance on top of it. The first year is when the survey misses surface, the crew turns over, the berth contract renews at a new price and the owner discovers which systems the previous owner deferred. Hire the management company before the purchase, not after it: a good manager will tell the buyer what the next three years cost. And price the yacht on its running cost, not its asking price. Two 100-footers at $12 million can cost $700,000 a year apart to run — different age, different engines, different crew, different home port — and the cheaper one to buy is often the more expensive one to own.

The owner in Monaco kept his yacht. He moved it to a cheaper winter berth, cut one crew position, and cruised slower. His second-year bill came in just under $1.2 million. He still calls it the most expensive thing he owns after his house.
