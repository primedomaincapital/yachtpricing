€4 Billion in Six Months: The Superyacht Market’s Quiet Comeback

SUPERYACHT MARKET 2026 PRICES  ·  4 min read

The docks at Port Hercule were full for the Monaco Yacht Show, September 23 to 26, with around 120 yachts assembled including dozens delivered during 2026. The mood on the quay was cautious, as it usually is. The numbers behind the show were not. The superyacht brokerage market had just recorded its strongest first half on record, and the third quarter was about to beat that. The market that spent 2024 cutting prices had spent 2026 selling boats — and the two facts are related in a way that matters for anyone trying to price one.

The half-year numbers

BoatPro, BOAT International’s market-intelligence platform, which tracks more than 13,000 superyachts worldwide, counted 281 yachts of at least 24 metres sold between January and June 2026, for a combined last-known asking value of €4.05 billion. That compares with 272 sales worth €3.61 billion in the same period of 2025 and 225 sales worth €2.21 billion in the first half of 2024. The growth was driven by bigger vessels: sales of yachts between 50 and 70 metres rose 52.9% against the first half of 2025, from 17 transactions to 26, and the 40-to-50-metre category grew 10%, while 30-to-40-metre sales fell 6%. The most valuable yacht sold in the period was Feadship’s 100.7-metre Moonrise, with an asking price of €325 million — roughly 8% of the half-year’s entire asking value in a single transaction. The average asking value of yachts sold reached €14.4 million, against €13.28 million in 2025 and €9.83 million in 2024. Final transaction prices are generally confidential, so every figure here is a last-known asking price — but the direction is unmistakable.

The third quarter kept going

Denison Yachting’s third-quarter report, published October 20, 2026, counted 126 superyachts of 24 metres and larger sold worldwide in Q3 2026, up 40% from 90 in Q3 2025 and 80% from 70 in Q3 2024. Disclosed asking prices across the quarter’s sales added up to about $1.02 billion, against roughly $829 million a year earlier, and the median price rose about 9%, from $5.1 million to $5.6 million. Through September, 416 superyachts had sold in 2026, up 13% from 368 at the same point last year. The median sale spent 317 days on the market, down from 343 days in Q3 2025 and 447 days in Q3 2024. Sunseeker recorded 10 third-quarter sales, up from one a year earlier, the most of any builder. The third quarter is normally the slowest stretch of the brokerage year, as summer cruising takes owners and yachts off the market. In 2026 it was the busiest third quarter of the last three years.

Northrop & Johnson’s data tells the same story with a different lens. Its second-quarter report counted 326 pre-owned superyachts sold in the first half of 2026, 8% fewer than a year earlier, for $3.51 billion — 15% more in value. Its review of January to August found transactions down 5.2% year on year to 421, while total sales value rose 19.7% to $4.84 billion, with the 40-to-50-metre bracket the standout, up from 43 sales to 59. Fewer boats, more money: the market consolidated around larger, newer, better-priced yachts, and the rest waited.

The other half of the story

The wait is visible in the price-reduction data. Northrop & Johnson recorded 1,415 price reductions between January and August 2026, worth $1.02 billion, up 7.1% on the year — 594 in the first quarter and 487 in the second. At July 1, it counted 2,157 pre-owned superyachts for sale, approximately 17% of the global fleet. BOAT International’s managing director Stewart Campbell attributed the improvement in transactions partly to a necessary adjustment in pricing that enabled deals to proceed. Knight Frank, citing BOAT International, reported that total superyacht sales value rose approximately 70% year on year during 2025. The comeback was not built on sellers holding firm. It was built on sellers blinking first — and buyers rewarding the ones who did.

The September wobble

There is a counter-reading, and September supplies it. Brokers sold 34 superyachts in September 2026, eleven more than in the same month last year, according to BoatPro — but those yachts were valued at €771.9 million against €961.1 million in September 2025, a fall of almost 20%. July had delivered €762.4 million across 52 yachts against €291.1 million a year earlier, and August €823.4 million across 40 sales against €414 million. September broke the run: more sales, less value, about €22.7 million a yacht against €41.8 million a year earlier. One month of data cannot answer whether that marks softer prices or simply a month in which smaller yachts sold — and September 2025, at €961.1 million, was more than double either of the two months before it. But it is a reminder that a market built on asking prices can look stronger than the money changing hands.

The order book adds a longer horizon to the same question. The number of yachts under construction has declined, but average size and tonnage have reached record levels, and leading shipyards carry construction commitments into 2028 and 2029. Builders in Italy account for approximately 52% of superyacht projects currently ordered or under construction worldwide. A buyer who wants a large yacht immediately has limited options unless they turn to the brokerage market or acquire a recently completed vessel — which is exactly the scarcity that pushed brokerage values up in the first place.

What the repricing window means

For buyers, the window is open but narrowing: realistic asking prices are transacting faster than at any point since 2024, with median time on market down 130 days in two years, while the inventory overhang — 17% of the fleet for sale — means the next price cut is always one listing away. For sellers, the lesson of 2026 is the opposite of the lesson of 2024: the boats that moved were the ones priced to move, and the premium now goes to condition and recency rather than to patience. The market did not recover because yachts got cheaper. It recovered because asking prices finally met the bids — and the bids, for the right boats, turned out to be substantial.
